# Develop episode titles and thumbnail hooks

A subject is not yet a hook. Choose an angle because it gives the intended viewer a clear reason to care and the episode delivers on it, not because that subject got the most airtime. Recent user-approved Weekly Claw titles and explicit preferences are the strongest style evidence. Current comparable shows can inform possibilities, but a competitor's wording or a sample of titles does not establish what performed well.

## Find and test angles

1. Read the final-cut topic ledger and inspect the relevant recording or verified transcript. Note timestamps, what the speakers actually say, why each topic might matter to the audience, and any uncertainty. A rough transcript or speculative interpretation is a lead to check against audio, not proof. Use airtime and opening prominence as clues, not automatic rankings.
2. Draft at least four substantially different title/thumbnail pairs when the source supports them. Explore different stories or genuine angles on the same story: a surprising development, a consequential question, a tension, a useful outcome, or a specific point of disagreement. Each listing title should work on its own; thumbnail text should add a clear complementary hook, not merely repeat it. A single broad label such as “AGENT FACTORIES” does not by itself tell a viewer what happened or why it matters.
3. For each pair, record the episode timestamp or verified source, the specific viewer payoff, and any material caveat. Ask: Would the intended viewer understand and care? Is the wording concrete, natural and specific to this episode? Does the recording support the implied claim, stakes and certainty? Does it promise something the episode actually delivers? Does the pair add information without redundancy? Could the same title fit dozens of unrelated episodes? Reject vague category labels, unexplained jargon, inflated claims, misleading questions, generic hype and empty curiosity gaps. Avoid implying measured click-through or predicting a winner without evidence.
4. Compare the strongest two or three distinct approaches against each other and recent accepted titles, explaining real tradeoffs briefly. Select the strongest supported pair or present alternatives when a creative choice needs the user's input; do not create a new approval gate. Preserve exact user-supplied title instructions. If the source cannot support four different angles, document the limitation instead of inventing them.
5. Keep the listing under 100 characters; retain established numbering when appropriate. Make artwork words as short as needed to read at mobile size, without forcing a word count. A multi-topic list is an option only if it improves the title; a single strong story, question or thesis may be better if the recording supports it. Names, numbers and claims must be verified. Record candidates, timestamps, caveats, decision and separate exact `episode_title` and `thumbnail_headline` values in the title rationale or release manifest.

Thumbnail image variations should test meaningful editorial alternatives when useful, rather than rendering the same unexamined phrase three times. Record each candidate's exact artwork wording and its corresponding proposed listing title. Once a particular image or listing is selected, preserve its existing approval and change scope; approving artwork alone does not silently change the listing title.
